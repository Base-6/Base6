Historical Evidence and Bias

This activity is about being a history detective: checking what a source is, who made it, what it says, and what it might be hiding. It usually takes about 35 to 45 minutes. You can jump around, skip a section, stop early, or come back later. Jay can check whatever you choose to send.

Step 1 of 7

Set up the history lab

Historians do not just ask, "What happened?" They also ask, "Who said this?", "Can it be checked?", and "Who benefits if people believe it?"

A source can be useful and biased at the same time. That is inconvenient, which is how history likes to behave.

Today you could practise

1. Matching source vocabulary.

2. Sorting fact from opinion.

3. Spotting bias in a factory owner source.

4. Reading a coin as evidence.

5. Comparing two interpretations.

Deep link for older learners: biased sources are not useless. They can reveal what someone wanted others to believe.
Step 2 of 7 · Predict first

Evidence word match

Before the matching game, predict this one: what does "bias" mean in history?

Clue: bias is when a source unfairly favours one side, person, group, or argument.

Match each term to its meaning

Deep link for older learners: "primary" does not automatically mean "more accurate"; it means closer in time to the event.
Step 3 of 7

Fact, opinion, and the factory owner

A fact can be checked. An opinion shows what someone thinks or feels. Bias often appears when opinions are dressed up to sound like facts.

Quick sort

"The factory was built in the year 1842."

Quick sort

"The King was a terrible leader who ruined the country."

"My factory is a wonderful place for children to work. The air is warm and clean. The children are happy and sing songs while they spin cotton. They are lucky I give them a job."

Written by Mr. Thomas Grind, Factory Owner, 1832.

Deep link for older learners: Mr. Grind's account is useful because it shows how an owner wanted factory work to appear, not because it proves factories were pleasant.
Step 4 of 7

Artefact analysis: the coin

Artefacts are objects from the past. A coin can send a message, especially when a king controls the design.

Tap parts of the coin to collect clues.

Deep link for older learners: objects can be propaganda. They show what powerful people wanted to communicate, not a neutral photograph of reality.
Step 5 of 7

Two versions of the same event

Different people can tell the story of the same event in very different ways. That does not always mean one is lying; it can mean they have different evidence, values, fears, or interests.

Source A

"The peasants were wicked rebels. They acted like wild dogs, destroying everything and murdering good, rich men."

Written by an English monk who owned a lot of land, 1381.

Source B

"The poor workers were starving and angry. The unfair taxes forced them to march to London to ask the King for help."

Written by a modern historian, 2015.

Source A

Source B

Bias check

Deep link for older learners: the monk's source is primary but highly hostile. Source B is secondary but may use lots of evidence to explain causes.
Step 6 of 7

Matching historical evidence

Match each evidence object to the best label. The drawings are simple on purpose; the thinking is the important bit.

Deep link for older learners: the type of evidence changes the questions you ask. A diary might reveal feelings; a castle might reveal defence, wealth, or control.
